Chapter 01Problem space

Four products, four boards, no single place to think.

Running a multi-product portfolio means a different work-item board, doc space, and stakeholder cast for every product. None of it aggregates at the personal level. Follow-ups mostly lived in email flags and memory instead, and some went stale. I also had about 70 bookmarks I'd stopped using.

Chapter 02Discovery

"Waiting on someone" is a different job than "I owe this."

Two weeks of structured self-observation surfaced the clearest finding: follow-up work has a distinct mental model from task work. It needs an owner and a source, and its own review cadence. No tool at the personal level modeled that.

✦ On capture friction
"Capture friction is the whole ballgame. If the tool of record demands too many fields, the thought just doesn't get captured."
Chapter 03Approach

The boring, approval-free path was the one that would ship.

I made follow-ups a first-class type, with an owner, a source, and its own board, rather than a tag bolted onto a task. And I built the whole thing as a single-file, offline, browser-storage app with zero backend, specifically so it needed no security review, no hosting, and no approval to start using immediately.

The trade-off, stated honestly: no cross-device sync, a hard storage ceiling, and no framework, in exchange for a tool that got used starting day one instead of never shipping at all.

Chapter 04Outcome

Replaced the bookmark bar and the sticky notes outright.

Chapter 06Lessons

Constraints are a design input, not an obstacle.

The cheapest tool that ships beats the ideal tool that doesn't. Zero-approval, zero-infra architecture was the actual reason this exists in daily use.

Model the user's mental model, not the domain's vocabulary. "Blocker" and "dependency" felt like statuses but were really reasons.

Watch your own behavior for a week before designing the data model. A proper browser database from day one would have avoided the storage ceiling.
